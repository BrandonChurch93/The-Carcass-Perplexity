Image 1 is a soft, low-resolution enlargement of one small part of a photograph taken from high orbit, looking straight down. It shows the far end of a long desert peninsula. The land bends and ends in a broad cape with a row of five small headlands and islets along its far edge, with turquoise shallows around it, in a deep blue ocean. Make the same view again as a sharp photograph. It is the same place seen by the same camera; nothing has moved.

Keep exactly as in Image 1: the position, size and direction of the peninsula and of the cape at its end; the mountain ridges and dry valleys on the land; the colours of rock, sand, shallows and deep water; the light; the clouds; the edges of the frame, so that the picture still joins the pictures around it.

Change one thing. The end of the cape is too neat to be real coast: five rounded lobes in a tidy row, as if pressed into clay. Give it the coast that the sea cuts over ages:
- The cape keeps its place, its size and its direction, but its edge is ragged: coves, low cliffs, sand spits, a drowned valley or two.
- Of the five lobes, the largest stays joined to the land as a stubby rocky headland. The other four stay where they are but become separate islands of clearly different sizes and irregular outlines, with narrow dark channels between them. The smallest is only a reef with a pale sand cay on it.
- A pale turquoise reef flat with sand shoals ties them loosely together under shallow water.
Nothing is symmetrical. No island is round. No two are alike.

Around the shore Image 1 has many small round brown islets, all alike, dotted evenly like pebbles. Remove them. In their place: fewer islands, cays and reef lines of very different sizes and irregular outlines.

Images 2 and 3 are real photographs taken from orbit. Use them only as the standard for how real desert coast, islands, reefs, shoals and sandbanks look from this height: flat, fine-grained, irregular, slightly hazy. Do not copy what they show.

The frame is about a thousand kilometres wide, and the cape is about two hundred kilometres across. No single hill, cliff, wave or building can be seen. Relief is flat to the eye: nothing is embossed, carved, sculpted or shiny. The sun is to the right and getting low. The open sea is smooth and deep blue.

A real photograph through the window of a spacecraft: natural colour, slightly muted by haze, sharp from edge to edge. No people, no buildings, no roads, no text, no labels, no borders. Not a sculpture, not a relief model, not an illustration.
