Image 1 is one part of a photograph taken from high orbit, looking almost straight down. It shows the middle of a desert continent that has the shape of a giant human body lying on its back in an ocean. At the left is the chest: long curved ridges on both sides of a black lava field that is crossed by a grey-green tidal strait, and at the far left edge the pale flat of the throat. In the middle is a shallow turquoise inland sea where the belly has sunk and flooded. Right of it lies a pale basin with dark red pools, and beyond that the two hips. Make the same photograph again: the same view from the same height.

Keep exactly as in Image 1: every coastline, including the whole outline of the turquoise inland sea and the channel that joins it to the ocean at the top; every curved ridge; the black lava field with its outline, its few orange lava rivers, its small bright vents and its thin smoke; the grey-green tidal strait with its sandbars; the colours of the inland sea and its shoals; the pale basin and the shape of its dark red pools; the mountains and plains of the hips at the right; the ocean, its shallows and its clouds at the top and bottom of the frame; the colours and the light; the edges of the frame, so that the picture still joins the pictures around it.

Two things inside the inland sea are wrong. Put them right and change nothing else.

1. The backbone. In Image 1 a chain of very small rock islands crosses the inland sea from the mouth of the strait at the left to the shore of the pale basin at the right. It is far too small to be seen for what it is. In its place, along the same line, put one narrow mountain range that crosses the whole inland sea from the mouth of the strait to the shore of the pale basin: the backbone of the body, laid bare where the belly sank. The range is about fifteen times as long as it is wide. It is a row of nine or ten blocky massifs, the vertebrae, each a blunt knot of bare rock with one short spur pointing out to each side into the water, like the bones of a spine seen from above. Each is joined to the next by a low narrow neck of land or parted from it by a narrow tidal gap. They are worn and unequal: some larger, some ground low, one split in two, no two alike, the gaps between them uneven, the line of the range bending slightly. Bare tan and grey rock like the other mountains of this continent, snow on the highest crests, pale shoals in the turquoise water at their feet. At a glance it is a spine; at a second look it is a weathered mountain range. It is not a wall, not a bridge and not a row of equal beads.

2. The storm. Over the inland sea Image 1 shows long white streaks of cloud lying side by side, with small white knots at their right ends. They look like smoke blowing off the lava field, and nobody sees a storm in them. Remove them completely, with their shadows, and show the turquoise water under them. Put one thunderstorm in their place, as a weather satellite sees a thunderstorm that stands against a mountain range, like the storms in Image 2 and Image 3. It stands against the lower flank of the backbone range, about the middle of its length, and it covers about a third of the length of the range. It is one dense, connected mass of storm cloud piled up against the range: broad at the middle, thinning along the range toward both ends, with a ragged lower edge out over the water. Its top is rough: four or five domed tops of unequal size crowd together and merge into each other. The low sun from the right lights its right side bright white. Its left side is blue-grey, and it lays a long dark shadow on the water and on the range to its left. The crest of the range holds the storm back: the summits stand in the upper edge of the cloud, and through two saddles torn banners of cloud pour across the range and are drawn out a short way toward the upper left, thinning to nothing. In the grey left side of the cloud two small patches glow a cold, pale blue-white from inside, where lightning is flashing under the tops: dim and soft-edged, no bolts, no rays. The upper half of the inland sea and both ends of the range are clear, with a few flocks of very small cumulus. Nothing streams out of the storm in long streaks, and it is not a ring, a puff or a burst.

Image 2 is a real satellite photograph of a chain of mountainous islands with cloud piled over them. Use it only as the standard for how a mountain chain in the sea and the cloud that stands on it look from orbit. Image 3 is a real satellite photograph of thunderstorms. Use it only as the standard for how the tops of thunderstorms look from orbit: firm, rough, bright on the sunny side, with long shadows. Do not copy the land or the sea either of them shows.

The frame is about two thousand kilometres wide; the inland sea is about five hundred kilometres across. Everything is seen from straight above: nothing is seen from the side, and no cloud is a round ball or a cauliflower in profile. Relief is flat to the eye: nothing is embossed, carved, sculpted or shiny.

A real photograph through the window of a spacecraft: natural colour, slightly muted by haze, sharp from edge to edge. No people, no buildings, no roads, no text, no labels, no borders. Not a sculpture, not a relief model, not an illustration.
