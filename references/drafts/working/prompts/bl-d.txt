Image 1 is one part of a photograph taken from high orbit, looking almost straight down. It shows the middle of a desert continent that has the shape of a giant human body lying on its back in an ocean. At the left is the chest: long curved ridges on both sides of a black lava field that is crossed by a grey-green tidal strait, and at the far left edge the pale flat of the throat. In the middle is a shallow turquoise inland sea where the belly has sunk and flooded. Right of it lies a pale basin with dark red pools, and beyond that the two hips. Make the same photograph again: the same view from the same height. Nothing on the ground has moved.

Keep exactly as in Image 1: every coastline; the course, curve and length of every curved ridge; the black lava field with its outline, its few orange lava rivers, its small bright vents and its thin smoke; the grey-green tidal strait with its sandbars; the outline of the turquoise inland sea and the channel that joins it to the ocean at the top; the pale basin and the shape of its dark red pools; the mountains and plains of the hips at the right; the ocean, its shallows and its clouds at the top and bottom of the frame; the colours and the light; the edges of the frame, so that the picture still joins the pictures around it.

Three things in Image 1 are wrong. Put them right and change nothing else.

1. The storm. Over the inland sea Image 1 shows five separate round puffs of cloud in a row, like balls of cotton wool, each with a glowing core. Seen from orbit no storm looks like that; they make the sea look like a pond. Remove all five, with their glows and their shadows. In their place put ONE large thunderstorm system seen from directly above, the way a weather satellite sees it, like the great white cloud mass in Image 2. It is a single continuous sheet of cloud with a ragged, irregular outline, roughly an oval, about half as long as the inland sea is wide. It lies over the middle of the island chain described in point 2, a little askew to it, and hides the islands beneath it; the chain shows again at both ends. Its top is flat, bright white and smooth as milk. Along its middle runs a rough band of many small, hard, bright lumps where the updraughts break through, each lump with its own tiny shadow. High wind draws the upper left edge of the sheet out into long thin fibres and half-transparent veils that fade away over the water. The lower right edge is firmer and scalloped. The sun is to the right and getting low, so the whole sheet lays one broad, dark shadow on the turquoise water along its left side. The shadow matters as much as the cloud. Around the system lie a few loose flocks of very small cumulus, uneven, with clear water between. At three places inside the sheet a small, soft patch of pale violet light shows through the cloud top where lightning is flashing underneath: each patch no bigger than one of the lumps, a blush of colour only, no bolts, no rays, nothing glowing outside the cloud.

2. The backbone. Through the middle of the inland sea, from the mouth of the strait at the left to the pale basin at the right, runs a chain of rock islands: the backbone of the titan, standing up out of the flooded belly. In Image 1 the islands are all alike, star-shaped and evenly spaced, like a string of beads. Make the chain a real drowned mountain range, like the island chain in Image 3: one line of islands along the same course, bending very slightly. The islands are unequal: two or three are long narrow ridges, several are short stubs, a few are only reefs and pale shoals, and the gaps of dark water between them are unequal too. Each island is rugged pale grey rock with snow along its crest and a rim of pale turquoise shallows; some have a short spur to one side. No two are alike; none is round or star-shaped. Seen from straight above, flat, with no side walls showing.

3. The ridges. The long curved ridges on both sides of the lava field are the ribs of the titan. In Image 1 they are too clean and too alike, like the teeth of a comb. Keep every ridge where it is, with the same course, the same curve and the same length, but weather them the way real fold mountains are weathered: crests uneven and notched; three or four of the ridges breached by dry valleys; two worn down into chains of low hills; pale fans of sand and gravel spreading out of the valleys between them; dune fields banked against some of them; snow only on the highest crests. Their widths and brightness are unequal. From this height they must still be seen at once as a rib cage.

Image 2 is a real satellite photograph of a large thunderstorm system. Use it only as the standard for how such a storm, its flat white top, its lumpy core, its feathered edge and its shadow look from orbit. Image 3 is a real satellite photograph of a chain of mountainous islands with cloud piled over them. Use it only as the standard for how an island chain, the cloud over it and open sea look from orbit. Do not copy the land or the sea either of them shows.

The frame is about two thousand kilometres wide; the inland sea is about four hundred kilometres across; the storm system is about two hundred and fifty kilometres long. No single hill, cliff or cloud tower can be picked out, and nothing is seen from the side. Relief is flat to the eye: nothing is embossed, carved, sculpted or shiny.

A real photograph through the window of a spacecraft: natural colour, slightly muted by haze, sharp from edge to edge. No people, no buildings, no roads, no text, no labels, no borders. Not a sculpture, not a relief model, not an illustration.
