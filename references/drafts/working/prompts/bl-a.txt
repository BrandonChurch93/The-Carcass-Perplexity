Image 1 is one part of a photograph taken from high orbit, looking almost straight down. It shows the middle of a desert continent that has the shape of a giant human body lying on its back in an ocean. At the left is the chest: long curved ridges on both sides of a black lava field, and at the far left edge the pale flat of the throat. In the middle is a shallow turquoise inland sea where the belly has sunk and flooded. Right of it lies a pale basin with dark red pools, and beyond that the two hips. Make the same photograph again: the same view from the same height. Nothing on the ground has moved.

Keep exactly as in Image 1: every coastline; every curved ridge; the outline of the black lava field; the outline of the turquoise inland sea and the channel that joins it to the ocean at the top; the pale basin and the shape of its dark red pools; the mountains and plains of the hips at the right; the ocean, its shallows and its clouds at the top and bottom of the frame; the colours and the light; the edges of the frame, so that the picture still joins the pictures around it.

Four things in Image 1 are wrong. Put them right and change nothing else.

1. The storm. Over the inland sea Image 1 shows a tall cloud seen from the side, as if from an aircraft. That is impossible from orbit. Remove it completely, with its white streak and its grey shadow. In its place put a line of thunderstorms seen from directly above, the way a weather satellite sees them. From above, a thunderstorm is a flat, bright white shield of cloud, round or oval, smooth as milk, with a soft feathered edge; near one end a small knot of harder, lumpy, brighter cloud marks where the updraught breaks through. Put four or five such shields, of clearly different sizes, in a loose row along the island chain described in point 2, some of them overlapping. The largest is about a quarter as wide as the inland sea. High wind draws thin, half-transparent streamers of cloud from them toward the upper left. A scatter of tiny cumulus dots lies around them. The sun is to the right and getting low, so every shield lays a dark, sharp-edged shadow on the water just to its left. The shadows matter as much as the clouds. Between the shields the islands and the water can still be seen.

2. The backbone. Through the middle of the inland sea, from the mouth of the strait at the left to the pale basin at the right, runs a single chain of rock islands: the backbone of the titan, standing up out of the flooded belly. Make it clear. About ten blocky islands of pale grey rock in one nearly straight row, close together, each a little different in size, each with a short spur to either side like a worn vertebra, narrow gaps of dark water between them, pale shoals round their feet, snow on their crests. They are weathered: one or two are split, one is only a reef. Seen from straight above, flat, with no side walls showing.

3. The strait. The thin straight grey line that crosses the black lava field, from the foot of the throat at the left to the inland sea, is a sea strait, and it is far too thin and too straight: it looks ruled. Make it a real tidal strait along the same line: an arm of the sea three to four times wider than now, grey-green water, pale grey tidal flats and sandbars along both edges, bending gently, pinching and widening, and opening like a funnel into the inland sea at its right end. It starts where it starts now.

4. The lava. On the black lava field the glow is a regular net of orange cracks, like veins. Replace the net with what a real eruption looks like from orbit: three or four small, intense, yellow-orange vents and, running from each, one or two thin, bright, wandering lava rivers that branch a little and fade to dull red and then to black. Most of the field is plain matte black and dark brown rock with a ragged edge. A thin brown-grey smoke haze drifts from the vents toward the upper left. No flames.

Also: the narrow channel at the top of the inland sea is plain water with pale sandbars. Remove the white mist that stands over it.

Images 2 and 3 are real satellite photographs of thunderstorms. Use them only as the standard for how thunderstorms, their flat white tops and their shadows look from orbit. Do not copy the land or the sea they show.

The frame is about two thousand kilometres wide; the inland sea is about four hundred kilometres across. No single hill, cliff or cloud tower can be picked out, and nothing is seen from the side. Relief is flat to the eye: nothing is embossed, carved, sculpted or shiny.

A real photograph through the window of a spacecraft: natural colour, slightly muted by haze, sharp from edge to edge. No people, no buildings, no roads, no text, no labels, no borders. Not a sculpture, not a relief model, not an illustration.
