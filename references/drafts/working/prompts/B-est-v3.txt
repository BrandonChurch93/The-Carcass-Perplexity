A wide 16:9 landscape frame from a big-budget natural-history film about an alien world. It must read as real location photography, not artwork: 24 mm lens, deep focus, natural light only, true lens perspective, fine physical surface detail, sea haze building with distance, slight film grain.

THE PLACE. A tidal shelf on a cold coast at low tide, soon after sunrise, sea fog burning off. The shelf is the exposed lung of a creature the size of a mountain range that died ages ago: bone-white, porous and honeycombed like weathered coral limestone, tide-stained and streaked with droppings. It is completely overgrown with animal life, not plants: wide carpets of crimson feathery gill-plumes like the crowns of giant tube worms, beds of pale barnacle-like cones, black-green ribbon weed, orange crusts. Set into the shelf are the open ends of the airways: enormous round throats twenty to sixty metres across. Each is a low broken collar of ivory cartilage, plainly built from stacked rings like the cut end of a giant ribbed hose, none taller than a house, all tilted at slightly different angles, with a dark hole in the middle. Every throat is exhaling at the same moment: a dense column of cold white vapour pours up out of each dark hole and leans the same way on the wind.

VIEWPOINT AND LAYOUT, asymmetric, nothing centred. Seen from standing height at the edge of a tide channel. The channel of pale teal seawater runs from the bottom centre away across the shelf, bending between the throats toward the far distance: the obvious route into the picture. On the right, about forty metres away, the nearest throat is seen clearly: its ringed outer wall, its wet rim, the dark opening, crimson plumes crowding the rim and bending outward in the rush of air. On the left bank in the foreground the crimson plume carpet comes right up to the viewer's feet, wet and glistening. More than twenty other exhaling throats stand at every distance along the channel, shrinking and paling into the fog. In the middle distance five tall pale striders wade the channel in a loose line: animals like giant sea spiders, each a body no bigger than a barrel carried eight metres up on eight very long, thin, jointed legs, with no neck and no head to speak of. A flock of hundreds of small dark flyers wheels through one distant column. At the far end of the channel, faint in the fog, the coast rises into hills, and out of the hillside lies the broken end of the main windpipe: one ribbed tube of pale cartilage hundreds of metres across, on its side like a fallen tower, eroded, cracked and overgrown, its dark mouth breathing out the fog bank itself.

LIGHT AND WEATHER. A low sun, back left, just breaking through the fog: warm pale-gold light edges every vapour column and lays long reflections on the wet shelf; shadows stay cold blue-grey. Colours: arterial crimson in broad masses, ivory bone, pale teal water, wet black, small patches of orange. The mood is cold, quiet and lethal.
