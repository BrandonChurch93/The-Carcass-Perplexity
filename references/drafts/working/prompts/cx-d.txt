Image 1 is a blurred layout map for a shot. It shows only where everything lies, and it must be obeyed for layout. It has no detail of its own: all detail must come from the real world. Where it shows soft rounded shading on the land, or cloud and smoke that look tall, ignore that: it is an artefact of the map. Images 2, 3, 4 and 5 are real photographs taken from spacecraft and satellites. They are the standard for how the finished picture must look. Image 2: the height of the camera, the haze and blue cast of the air, the curved edge of the planet, how small cloud is. Image 3: bare pale rock in a dark sea seen from orbit: ragged capes, fine valleys with thin shadows, no relief standing up. Image 4: stained sea water (the milky swirls of a bloom) and a real band of frontal cloud. Image 5: thunderstorms seen from straight above: flat, round, white anvil tops with soft edges, a shadow beside each, among fine cloud. Do not copy what Images 2 to 5 show.

Make the real photograph that the mock-up was planning: one photograph taken from a spacecraft three thousand kilometres above an alien ocean world, looking straight down at an entire continent, late in the day. Natural colour, cold and muted, wide 16:9 frame.

The continent is a carcass: the dead body of a titan of human shape that fell on its back ages ago, rotted where it lay, dried out and weathered into land. Its outline says corpse; its surface says continent. At the first glance a viewer must see a huge dead human body, long decayed. At the second glance they must believe it is a real continent photographed from space.

Keep the layout of Image 1 exactly. The same outline of land, with every part of the body in the same place, at the same size and at the same angle: the skull at the left with its two eye sockets, nose pit, teeth and fallen jaw; the neck; the shoulders; the arm flung up with its clawed hand; the other arm lying down and out; the open rib cage with the strait down its middle; the backbone crossing open sea where the belly was; the hips; the straight leg and its foot; the bent leg. The same pale drowned shoals, the same stained water, the same edge of the planet in the top left corner, the same spiral storm in the lower right corner, the same sunglint at the right. Keep the dark leathery hide where Image 1 has it and the bare bone where Image 1 has it.

Change these things, and only these.

1. Relief. The body is four thousand kilometres long. From this height land is almost flat. Nothing stands up, nothing is embossed, no bone is a rounded tube lying on the water. A bone is a mountain belt: a band of hundreds of fine wrinkled ridges and valleys, with snow on the highest crests, pale scree and fans at its feet and a ragged coast. Shadows are thin: a hairline of dark on the side of each ridge away from the sun. The hollows are dark because they hold deep water or dark rock, not because a tall wall shades them.

2. Surface. No smooth, sculpted, polished or plaster surface anywhere. Bone country is worn ivory-grey limestone: fine branching valleys, dry river beds like hairlines, pale salt and sand in the basins, snow on the crests. Hide country is low, dark plateau: charcoal brown, rust and dull oxblood, cracked by canyons into polygons like dried mud, holed and torn, its edges crumbling into the sea in cliffs and stacks. Coasts everywhere are ragged with capes, fjords, spits and chains of islets, and fringed with thin pale shallows.

3. Regularity. Nothing is as neat as an anatomy chart. No two ribs have the same width, length or spacing; several are snapped short, two have fallen flat, some have gone under and show only as shoals. The teeth are a worn, gapped row of stacks and stumps. Finger and toe bones are crooked chains of capes and islets of unequal length. The two bones of a forearm or shin are unequal ranges that touch, part and break.

4. The storm over the backbone. Remove the tall bulging cloud tower seen from the side. In its place put what a satellite sees, as in Image 5: a sheet of low grey-white cloud dammed against the lower side of the backbone range, and standing in it, against the middle of the range, one thunderstorm seen from straight above: a flat, round, brilliant white anvil about as wide as the hip massif is tall, with a soft fibrous edge, a small lumpy boss at its centre, a blue-grey shadow thrown to its left, and a faint violet glow inside it. No drawn lightning bolt.

5. The lava. Keep the matte black lava field inside the upper half of the rib cage. Shrink the glow to one small ember-orange spot with a few hair-thin orange cracks. Its fume is one thin, flat, grey-brown streak blown hundreds of kilometres toward the top of the frame, widening and fading, with a thin shadow beside it. Nothing billows.

6. The airways at the throat. Dark round shafts of different sizes in a pale tidal shelf beside the neck bones, with a wine-red stain round them. Their cold white vapour lies flat on the surface as thin white streamers drifting toward the top of the frame.

7. Cloud everywhere. All cloud is seen from above and lies flat: fine stipple, thin streets, long curved bands, patches of fog in bays, each puff far smaller than a fingertip of the body, each with its small shadow on the sea. No cloud or smoke is seen from the side. No cotton balls.

8. The sea. Deep water is nearly black, cold slate blue. The sunglint at the right is a smooth dull pewter sheen with faint slicks and streaks in it and no visible waves or ripples. Keep the pale jade-grey shoals, the sick green and ochre swirls and the rust-red plume that drains from the red salt pan in the hips.

The light is the light of late afternoon coming from the right through thin haze, flat and cold; the far left of the frame is dimming toward dusk.

No people, no text, no labels, no borders, no spacecraft parts. Not a sculpture, not a toy, not a relief model, not an anatomy chart, not an illustration: a real photograph of a real world.
