A wide 16:9 landscape frame from a big-budget natural-history film about an alien world, shot on location. It must read as real photography of a real place, not artwork: 28 mm lens, deep focus, true lens perspective, natural light only, sea fog building with distance, slight film grain, restrained natural colour.

THE PLACE. A vast tidal shelf on a cold coast at low tide, just after sunrise, sea fog burning off. The shelf is the exposed lung of a creature the size of a mountain range that died ages ago: pale bone-white rock, porous and honeycombed like weathered coral limestone, terraced, tide-stained, cut by shallow pale teal channels and pools. Set into the shelf are the open ends of its airways: round throats from five to sixty metres across. Most are only a dark hole with a low broken lip; the larger ones keep a collar of ivory cartilage built from stacked rings, eroded and incomplete. The swell drives air through them and they exhale together: a column of cold white vapour pours from each and leans the same way on the wind. Animal life, not plants, grows where the water and the breath reach: bands of crimson feathery gill-plumes along the waterline of the channels and around the throat rims, black-green weed lower down, orange crusts on the dry high rock, pale barnacle-like cones in between. The rest is bare wet bone.

VIEWPOINT AND LAYOUT, asymmetric. Seen from a ledge about six metres above a tide channel. The channel runs from the bottom centre away across the shelf in a slow S toward the far distance: the route. On the right, sixty metres away, the nearest large throat: its ringed collar, its dark opening, crimson plumes on its rim bent outward by the rush of air, its vapour column rising out of frame. In the lower left corner the edge of the ledge, wet, with one patch of crimson plumes and a tide pool. Across the middle distance four or five more throats of very different sizes, one of them quiet with only a wisp, and far beyond them dozens of thin vapour columns dissolving into a bank of fog. Three hundred metres away a loose group of five striders wades the channel: pale animals like giant sea spiders, each a small body carried eight metres up on very long thin jointed legs, small in the frame, each in a different attitude, their reflections and long shadows on the wet shelf. A flock of small dark flyers turns through one far column. At the far end, kilometres away and half lost in the fog, the coast rises into snow-streaked hills, and at their foot lies the broken end of the main windpipe: one ribbed tube of pale cartilage hundreds of metres across, on its side, collapsed and overgrown with crimson and orange, fog pouring from its dark mouth.

RESTRAINT. This is a calm, wide establishing frame. The land and the weather are the subject. Big simple shapes; wide areas of plain wet bone-white rock, shallow water and fog. Life is sparse and small in the frame and sits in zones where it would really grow, never spread evenly over everything. Nothing is repeated: every throat, vapour column, strider and patch of growth differs from the others in size, shape, state and angle, and some are broken, some empty, some half hidden.

LIGHT AND WEATHER. Backlit. The low sun is ahead and to the left, a pale disc in the fog. Everything facing the viewer is in soft cool shadow with bright warm rims; the vapour columns glow where the sun passes through them; a path of sun glitter lies on the channel; long shadows fall toward the viewer. Colours: ivory and grey-white rock, pale teal water, deep crimson in bands, wet black, small touches of orange, pale gold light. The mood is cold, quiet and lethal.

It must feel like a place you could walk into and keep walking: firm ground at your feet, a readable route into the middle distance, far landmarks dissolving in the air, the land plainly continuing beyond every edge of the frame. Nothing posed, nothing centred, no painterly brushwork, no illustration or game-art styling. No people, no buildings or ruins, no cameras, drones or equipment, no text, no watermark, no border.
