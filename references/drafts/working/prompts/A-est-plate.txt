Two reference images are supplied, with different jobs.
Image 1 is a real photograph. Use it only as the standard for how the new picture is rendered: how a real camera records light, haze, exposure and colour; soft highlights; detail that softens with distance; large plain, unremarkable surfaces. Do not copy its subject, its layout or anything in it.
Image 2 is a rough, blurred layout sketch of the place to photograph. Use it only for where things are: the viewpoint, the horizon, the route, the position and size of each landmark. Do not copy its surface texture, its sharpness or its rendering. Invent every surface fresh, as a camera would record real materials.

Make a new 16:9 photograph: one frame from a big-budget natural-history film about an alien world, shot on location with a large-format cinema camera and a 24 mm lens. It must be indistinguishable from real location photography.

THE PLACE. The inside of the windpipe of a creature the size of a mountain range that died ages ago, now a limestone-like cave passage about two hundred metres high and wide, running away from the viewer and curving out of sight. One part of the roof fell in long ago, leaving a single ragged skylight. Daylight comes down through it into wet air as one broad soft glow with ragged edges, and under it a patch of rainforest has grown on the rubble: tree ferns, pale-trunked trees of different heights with dark red crowns, one dead bare tree, undergrowth. A broad waterfall drops from the lip of the skylight behind the trees into a plunge pool hidden in spray, and the pool feeds a shallow river that runs toward the viewer over gravel bars and small rapids: the route. The cave walls are ordinary wet cave rock, dark, with flowstone, drip stains and ledges. Along the left wall, half buried in flowstone and moss, run the stumps of a few enormous pale cartilage hoops, each as thick as a house, each different, one fallen; they read as part of the cave, like buried ribs, and they do not frame the picture. Away from the skylight the cave is in deep cool shadow.

LIFE. Near the forest, far from the viewer, a small herd of heavy, thick-skinned, eyeless grazing animals: two drinking at the river's edge, one lying on a gravel bar, one half hidden in the ferns, a young one beside an adult. The largest is no taller than a twentieth of the frame height. On the mossy ground in the near foreground, to one side, small and easy to miss: the moss-covered bones of one of them, with a few pale leathery puffball bladders of different sizes growing from it, one collapsed. A few small flyers as specks in the lit air.

LIGHT. Only the daylight from the one skylight: bright and soft on the forest and the spray, green bounce on the nearest wall, a dim reflection on the river, deep shadow everywhere else. Colours: deep green, wet near-black rock, pale bone, a little dark red.

WHAT MAKES IT REAL. One light source and one weather, consistent everywhere. Most of the frame is plain: dark wet rock, shadow, water and mist. Surfaces have no repeating pattern, no scales, no crackle and no carved detail. Nothing is evenly spaced and nothing is repeated; every object differs in size, shape and state, and some are broken, half hidden or cut off by the edge of the frame. Animals are small in the frame, in different poses and facing different ways, some partly hidden; none is posed side-on for the camera. Far things are paler, softer and lower in contrast than near things. Natural exposure with gentle highlights and open shadows, no added sharpening, no glow added in post, restrained colour, fine film grain.

The land plainly continues beyond every edge of the frame. No people, no buildings, paths or railings, no equipment, no text, no watermark, no border.
